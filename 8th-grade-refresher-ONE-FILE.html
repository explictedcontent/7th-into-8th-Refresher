💡 Light & Shadow

Pick a light source (say, a lamp on the left). Everything facing the light is bright; everything turned away is dark. On a round object you'll see 5 areas:

PartWhat it is
HighlightThe brightest spot, where light hits most directly
LightThe lit side, a medium-light value
Core shadowThe darkest band, on the edge turning away from the light
Reflected lightA faint glow on the shadow side, bounced from the table
Cast shadowThe dark shape the object throws onto the table
💡
Key idea: the core shadow is not at the very edge — there's a thin strip of reflected light beyond it. That little detail is what makes objects look real.

🟠 Shading a Sphere — step by step

  1. Lightly draw a circle and decide where the light comes from.
  2. Leave a small white highlight on the light side.
  3. Add a core shadow as a curved band on the opposite side.
  4. Blend the light area smoothly between the highlight and the core shadow.
  5. Soften a sliver of reflected light just past the core shadow.
  6. Drop a cast shadow on the ground, darkest right under the ball.

📐 Perspective Basics

TermWhat it is
Horizon lineThe line at your eye level, where sky meets ground
Vanishing pointThe dot on the horizon where everything shrinks to nothing
Orthogonal linesThe slanted lines that run from an object back to the vanishing point
💡
Objects look bigger up close and smaller as they near the vanishing point — that shrinking is what your eye reads as depth.

📦 Draw a Box in 3-D

  1. Draw a horizon line with a vanishing point on it.
  2. Draw a square off to one side — the front of the box.
  3. Lightly connect each corner of the square to the vanishing point.
  4. Mark the back of the box with a smaller square along those lines.
  5. Trace the real edges, erase the extras — a solid 3-D cube!

Pick one light direction (say, top-left) and every round object shows these five things, always in the same order from bright to dark:

🌑🌕 Value scale & light direction

A value scale is a strip that steps from white to black. Drawing one trains your hand to make smooth, believable shadows. In your head, read this 5-step scale from lightest to darkest:

  • ⚪ White
  • 🌑 Light gray
  • 🌘 Medium gray
  • 🌑 Dark gray
  • ⚫ Black

If the light comes from the top-left, the highlight sits on the side, and the core shadow + cast shadow fall toward the side. The shadow is always on the side .

🎡
This is where last lesson pays off: on the blending wheel, slide up for the highlight (a tint) and down for the core and cast shadows (shades) — same color, different value.

🖌️ Studio A · Shade a 3D sphere

Let's make a flat circle look like a real ball. Work in this order:

  • 1. Pick a color on the wheel and lightly fill a big circle (that's your midtone).
  • 2. Slide the side bar down for a darker shade and paint the core shadow as a curved band on the lower-right.
  • 3. Slide up for a light tint and dab a highlight on the upper-left.
  • 4. Switch to ink/black and paint a cast shadow on the ground, stretching to the lower-right.
  • 5. Save it when you're proud of it!

🍎 Studio B · A tiny still-life

Now use the same trick on a little scene. Draw two or three solid objects sitting on a table — maybe a ball, a box, and a cup — and give each one a highlight, a core shadow, and a cast shadow that all point the same direction (because there's one light). Matching shadow directions is the secret that makes a drawing look real.

📏
Composition tip — the rule of thirds: imagine the canvas split into 9 squares by two lines across and two down. Place your main object near a spot where the lines cross, not dead-center. It instantly looks more interesting.

📏 The 5 rules of face proportion

RuleWhere it goes
1. EyesHalfway down the head — not near the top (beginners always draw them too high)
2. Eye spacingThere is room for one more eye between the two eyes
3. Bottom of noseHalfway between the eyes and the chin
4. MouthAbout one-third of the way down from the nose to the chin
5. EarsTop lines up with the eyebrows, bottom with the bottom of the nose
💡
The biggest fix for beginner faces: drop the eyes to the middle of the head. The forehead is bigger than you think!

➗ Ratios & Rates

A ratio compares two amounts, like 2 cats to 3 dogs (2:3). Simplify it like a fraction by dividing both sides by the same number: 6:9 = 2:3.

A rate compares different units (miles per hour). A unit rate is "per 1": 120 miles in 2 hours = 60 miles per hour.

💡
To get a unit rate, divide: 120 ÷ 2 = 60 mph. $6 ÷ 3 apples = $2 per apple.

％ Percents

A percent means "out of 100." To find a percent of a number, turn the percent into a decimal and multiply:

Example

25% of 80 → 0.25 × 80 = 20.   10% of 50 → 0.10 × 50 = 5.

🛍️
Discount: a $40 shirt at 25% off → the discount is 25% of 40 = $10, so it costs $30.

BranchWho's in itWhat it does
LegislativeCongress = the Senate + the House of RepresentativesMakes the laws
ExecutiveThe President + Vice President + CabinetCarries out (enforces) the laws
JudicialThe Supreme Court + other federal courtsInterprets the laws — decides what they mean and if they're constitutional
💡
Quick way to remember: Legislative = Legislate (make). Executive = Execute (do). Judicial = Judge (decide).

🔄 Checks & Balances

Each branch can limit the others, so no branch becomes too powerful:

  • The President can veto (reject) a bill from Congress.
  • Congress can override a veto with a 2/3 vote — and can impeach officials.
  • The Courts can declare a law unconstitutional and cancel it.
  • Congress approves the judges the President picks.
⚖️
This back-and-forth is called checks and balances — the heart of how the Constitution shares power.

📜 How a Bill Becomes a Law

  1. An idea is written up as a bill.
  2. It's introduced in Congress (the House or the Senate).
  3. A committee studies, debates, and may change it.
  4. Both the House and the Senate vote — it must pass both.
  5. It goes to the President.
  6. The President signs it → it becomes a law. (Or vetoes it.)
  7. If vetoed, Congress can still pass it with a 2/3 override vote.

📜 The Constitution

The Constitution is the plan and rulebook for the U.S. government — it sets up the three branches and the laws they must follow. It was written in 1787 and is still the highest law in the country.

An amendment is an official change or addition to the Constitution. There are 27 of them so far.

💡
The Constitution can be changed — but on purpose it's hard to do, so rights stay protected over time.

🗽 The Bill of Rights

The Bill of Rights is the name for the first 10 amendments. They protect the freedoms of the people. The famous 1st Amendment protects five freedoms:

  • Freedom of speech
  • Freedom of religion
  • Freedom of the press
  • Freedom of assembly (gathering peacefully)
  • The right to petition (ask the government to fix things)

📜 The short story

In the 1760s and 1770s, Britain taxed its American colonies to pay for war debts — on things like tea, paper, and stamps. The colonists had no representatives in the British Parliament, so they protested: “No taxation without representation!”

Anger boiled over at the Boston Tea Party (1773), when colonists dumped British tea into the harbor. Fighting began at Lexington and Concord (1775). On July 4, 1776, the colonies approved the Declaration of Independence, mostly written by Thomas Jefferson. George Washington led the Continental Army, and after years of war the Americans won the final battle at Yorktown (1781).

How to do it: A memory trick: 13 = free, 14 = citizen, 15 = vote. They go in order: first you’re free, then a citizen, then you can vote.

Read this:

When the Civil War ended in 1865, the country had to rebuild — a time called Reconstruction. Three new amendments were added: the 13th ended slavery, the 14th made formerly enslaved people citizens with equal protection under the law, and the 15th said the right to vote couldn’t be denied because of race. But progress didn’t last. Southern states passed Jim Crow laws that segregated (separated) Black and white people and blocked Black citizens from voting. Nearly a century later, the Civil Rights Movement fought back. Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat in 1955. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. led peaceful marches and gave his “I Have a Dream” speech in 1963. In 1964 the Civil Rights Act finally outlawed segregation.
