🌈 Color Theory Basics

The color wheel

  • Primary colors — red, yellow, blue. Can't be mixed from others.
  • Secondary colors — orange, green, purple. Mix two primaries.
  • Complementary colors sit opposite (like blue & orange). Side by side, they pop!

Warm vs. Cool

Warm 🔥 reds, oranges, yellows — cozy, exciting, close.
Cool ❄️ blues, greens, purples — calm, quiet, far away.

  • My 5 boxes go evenly from lightest to darkest

💡 Light & Shadow

Pick a light source (say, a lamp on the left). Everything facing the light is bright; everything turned away is dark. On a round object you'll see 5 areas:

PartWhat it is
HighlightThe brightest spot, where light hits most directly
LightThe lit side, a medium-light value
Core shadowThe darkest band, on the edge turning away from the light
Reflected lightA faint glow on the shadow side, bounced from the table
Cast shadowThe dark shape the object throws onto the table
💡
Key idea: the core shadow is not at the very edge — there's a thin strip of reflected light beyond it. That little detail is what makes objects look real.

🟠 Shading a Sphere — step by step

  1. Lightly draw a circle and decide where the light comes from.
  2. Leave a small white highlight on the light side.
  3. Add a core shadow as a curved band on the opposite side.
  4. Blend the light area smoothly between the highlight and the core shadow.
  5. Soften a sliver of reflected light just past the core shadow.
  6. Drop a cast shadow on the ground, darkest right under the ball.

One-Point Perspective: Draw 3-D Space 📐

Perspective is the trick that makes a flat page look deep — like you could step right into it. It all starts with a single dot.

✏️ Warm-up · Set the stage

In the studio below, do this fast:

  • Draw one straight horizon line across the middle
  • Put a single dot on it — the vanishing point
  • Draw 5 lines that all start at the dot and spread out like sun rays

📐 Perspective Basics

TermWhat it is
Horizon lineThe line at your eye level, where sky meets ground
Vanishing pointThe dot on the horizon where everything shrinks to nothing
Orthogonal linesThe slanted lines that run from an object back to the vanishing point
💡
Objects look bigger up close and smaller as they near the vanishing point — that shrinking is what your eye reads as depth.

📦 Draw a Box in 3-D

  1. Draw a horizon line with a vanishing point on it.
  2. Draw a square off to one side — the front of the box.
  3. Lightly connect each corner of the square to the vanishing point.
  4. Mark the back of the box with a smaller square along those lines.
  5. Trace the real edges, erase the extras — a solid 3-D cube!

🧭 Part 2: Order of Operations (PEMDAS)

  1. Parentheses first
  2. Exponents
  3. Multiply & Divide — left to right
  4. Add & Subtract — left to right
⚠️
Watch out! Multiply and divide are a team — do whichever comes first, left to right. Same for add and subtract.
Worked example

Solve 6 + 2 × (5 − 1)²

  1. Parentheses: 5 − 1 = 4 → 6 + 2 × 4²
  2. Exponent: 4² = 16 → 6 + 2 × 16
  3. Multiply: 2 × 16 = 32 → 6 + 32
  4. Add: 6 + 32 = 38

Get x alone by doing the inverse operation to both sides.

Two-step rule: undo + or − first, then undo × or ÷ (PEMDAS backwards).

➗ Ratios & Rates

A ratio compares two amounts, like 2 cats to 3 dogs (2:3). Simplify it like a fraction by dividing both sides by the same number: 6:9 = 2:3.

A rate compares different units (miles per hour). A unit rate is "per 1": 120 miles in 2 hours = 60 miles per hour.

💡
To get a unit rate, divide: 120 ÷ 2 = 60 mph. $6 ÷ 3 apples = $2 per apple.

％ Percents

A percent means "out of 100." To find a percent of a number, turn the percent into a decimal and multiply:

Example

25% of 80 → 0.25 × 80 = 20.   10% of 50 → 0.10 × 50 = 5.

🛍️
Discount: a $40 shirt at 25% off → the discount is 25% of 40 = $10, so it costs $30.

Subject + Predicate

Subject = who/what it's about. Predicate = what they do or are (has the verb).

"My older sister paints every weekend."

ProblemWhat it isFix

🎭 The Types of Figurative Language

TypeWhat it doesExample
SimileCompares two things using like or as"as busy as a bee"
MetaphorSays one thing is another (no like/as)"Time is a thief"
PersonificationGives human traits to non-human things"The wind whispered"
HyperboleHuge exaggeration for effect"I could eat a horse"
IdiomA phrase that doesn't mean its literal words"break a leg" (= good luck)
OnomatopoeiaA word that imitates a soundbuzz, splash, pop
AlliterationSame beginning sound, repeated"wild and windy"
💡
Simile vs. metaphor: a simile uses like or as ("brave as a lion"); a metaphor says it is ("she is a lion").

Changes of state (adding or removing heat)

  • Melting: solid → liquid (ice → water)

  • Evaporation / boiling: liquid → gas (water → steam)
  • Condensation: gas → liquid (steam → droplets on a mirror)
💡

🫀 The Heart & Blood

Your heart is a muscle about the size of your fist. It pumps blood every second of your life — roughly 60–100 beats a minute at rest.

VesselIts job
ArteriesCarry blood away from the heart (oxygen-rich)
VeinsCarry blood back to the heart
CapillariesTiny vessels where blood trades oxygen & nutrients with cells
💡
Memory tip: Arteries carry blood Away. Blood carries oxygen and nutrients to cells and hauls away waste like carbon dioxide.

🫁 The Lungs & Gas Exchange

When you breathe in, air travels down your trachea into your lungs, all the way to millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli.

  1. You breathe in oxygen from the air.
  2. In the alveoli, oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out.
  3. You breathe out carbon dioxide, the waste gas.
  4. The diaphragm (a muscle under your lungs) pulls down to help you inhale.
🤝
That's why exercise makes you breathe harder: your muscles need more oxygen, so your heart beats faster and your lungs work harder to keep up.

The U.S. Constitution splits the government into three branches so no single person or group gets too much power. Here's how it works.

🏛️ Warm-up · Who does what?

The branch that makes laws → , the branch that carries out laws → , the branch that interprets laws → .

⚖️ The Three Branches

BranchWho's in itWhat it does
LegislativeCongress = the Senate + the House of RepresentativesMakes the laws
ExecutiveThe President + Vice President + CabinetCarries out (enforces) the laws
JudicialThe Supreme Court + other federal courtsInterprets the laws — decides what they mean and if they're constitutional
💡
Quick way to remember: Legislative = Legislate (make). Executive = Execute (do). Judicial = Judge (decide).

🔄 Checks & Balances

Each branch can limit the others, so no branch becomes too powerful:

  • The President can veto (reject) a bill from Congress.
  • Congress can override a veto with a 2/3 vote — and can impeach officials.
  • The Courts can declare a law unconstitutional and cancel it.
  • Congress approves the judges the President picks.
⚖️
This back-and-forth is called checks and balances — the heart of how the Constitution shares power.

📜 How a Bill Becomes a Law

  1. An idea is written up as a bill.
  2. It's introduced in Congress (the House or the Senate).
  3. A committee studies, debates, and may change it.
  4. Both the House and the Senate vote — it must pass both.
  5. It goes to the President.
  6. The President signs it → it becomes a law. (Or vetoes it.)
  7. If vetoed, Congress can still pass it with a 2/3 override vote.
